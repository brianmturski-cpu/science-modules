The sodium-potassium pump

Active transport moves particles from low concentration to high concentration, which is against the gradient (uphill). That takes energy. The cell pays with ATP, the molecule that carries energy in the cell. The sodium-potassium pump (Na⁺/K⁺ pump) is a protein in the cell membrane that runs this way all the time.

Cholera and oral rehydration

Patient file. A 14-year-old who weighs 50 kg has cholera. Cholera is caused by the bacterium Vibrio cholerae, which spreads in dirty water. The patient has already lost about 2.5 L of body fluid. The clinic has no IV supplies. Every hour, the patient can drink 750 mL. Your job: test which drink keeps the patient alive.

The picture shows one cell of the small intestine lining, between the gut and the blood. Three membrane proteins matter here:

  • SGLT1 (sodium-glucose cotransporter) on the gut side carries Na⁺ and glucose into the cell together. Na⁺ flows in down its gradient, and that flow drags glucose in with it. This is cotransport, also called secondary active transport: it runs on the Na⁺ gradient that the pump makes.
  • The Na⁺/K⁺ pump on the blood side pushes Na⁺ out of the cell into the blood. This keeps Na⁺ low inside the cell, so SGLT1 keeps working.
  • CFTR is a chloride (Cl⁻) channel on the gut side. Cholera toxin gets into the cell and keeps a signal molecule called cAMP switched on, so CFTR stays open. Cl⁻ pours into the gut, Na⁺ follows between the cells, and water follows the salt by osmosis. That is the watery diarrhea.

How the model works: 750 mL of drink goes into the gut each hour. SGLT1 can only use the Na⁺ and glucose that arrive together in the drink, in matched amounts, so whichever runs out first limits it. Cholera toxin does not change SGLT1. It opens CFTR and slows the cell’s other salt carriers, and the flooded gut moves fluid along so fast that less plain water and extra sugar get taken up. Sugar the gut cannot take up stays in the gut and holds water there. Real patients are more complicated.

Cystic fibrosis: a protein that never arrives

Cystic fibrosis (CF) is a genetic disease. It comes from changes in the gene for CFTR, the same Cl⁻ channel you saw in the cholera tab. In airway cells, CFTR lets Cl⁻ out onto the airway surface. Water follows by osmosis and keeps the mucus thin, so tiny hairs called cilia can sweep it (and germs) out of the lungs.

CFTR is a membrane protein, so it follows the protein factory path from Block 2.2: ribosome on the rough ER → ER quality check → Golgi → vesicle → cell membrane. The ER quality check is how the cell inspects new proteins: a protein that is folded wrong is sent to the proteasome (a protein shredder) instead of being shipped.

The gene versions

  • ΔF508 (also written F508del): the protein is missing one amino acid (F, phenylalanine) at position 508. It folds wrong, and the ER quality check destroys almost all of it. Nearly 90% of people with CF have at least one copy.
  • G551D: one amino acid at position 551 is changed (G, glycine, becomes D, aspartic acid). The protein folds fine and reaches the membrane, but its gate stays closed most of the time. About 4% of people with CF in the U.S. have it.

The drugs

  • Ivacaftor is a potentiator: it helps the CFTR gate stay open longer. Approved in the U.S. in 2012 for people with G551D.
  • Elexacaftor and tezacaftor are correctors: they help the protein fold, so more of it passes the quality check. They are given with ivacaftor as one treatment, approved in 2019 for people age 12 and up with at least one F508del copy, and later for more people.
  • These drugs do not cure CF and do not work for every mutation.

Sweat test: CFTR in sweat glands normally pulls Cl⁻ back out of sweat. Without working CFTR, sweat stays very salty. Cystic Fibrosis Foundation guidelines: sweat chloride of 60 mmol/L or more is consistent with CF; 30–59 is intermediate; under 30 means CF is unlikely.

Bulk transport sorter

Some things are too big, or needed in too large an amount, to go through a protein carrier or channel. The cell moves them in bulk, using pieces of its own membrane. Vesicles are small membrane bubbles that carry cargo. Bending the membrane and moving vesicles uses energy from ATP, so all four kinds are active processes.

Phagocytosis (“cell eating”): the membrane wraps around a large particle, like a whole cell, and pulls it in.
Pinocytosis (“cell drinking”): the membrane pinches in small drops of outside fluid, with whatever is dissolved in it.
Receptor-mediated endocytosis: receptor proteins grab one specific molecule first, then that patch of membrane folds in.
Exocytosis: a vesicle fuses with the membrane and empties its cargo outside the cell.
